# Confessions of an IT Manager (Phil Factor, 2009)

TL;DR: Humorous collection of essays (306pp, 2nd ed., Simple Talk Publishing, ISBN 978-1-906434-18-2) by Phil Factor — a pseudonymous database expert with 25+ years experience. Written in the style of anecdotes, parodies, and satire, the book delivers hard-earned wisdom about IT management, software project failure, hiring, vendor relations, and the absurdities of corporate IT culture. Rooted in real experience; the humor is the delivery vehicle for practical insight. All 11 parts (complete book) ingested.

## Author

**Phil Factor** (pseudonym, real identity kept confidential — "to protect the guilty") — database practitioner with 25+ years of experience; columnist for Simple Talk magazine; reviewer of Neil Davidson's *Don't Just Roll the Dice*. Has reportedly been shouted at by Bill Gates at an exhibition in the 1980s [^p01].

---

## Section I: On Learning to Love your Manager

**Training Your IT Manager**: IT managers are often technically illiterate and respond best when technical concerns are reframed in language that maps to their status, career interests, and avoidance of blame. Understanding this social dynamic is not cynicism — it is the necessary prerequisite for getting anything done [^p01].

**The Septic Tank principle**: in corporate life, the largest, most unpleasant things float to the top. Senior management in IT departments tends to be furthest removed from operational reality [^p02].

**Workplace intelligence gathering**: a recurring theme across multiple chapters is that information — who knows what about whom — is a strategic resource. The anecdote of "Bunnikins" illustrates how a compromising email chain can reshape organizational power more decisively than any technical argument [^p02].

**Irregular verbs for IT managers**: a sardonic list illustrating how the same behavior is described differently depending on who is doing it. Example pattern: "I am a visionary thinker; you are a loose cannon; he is a dangerously unstable obsessive" [^p01].

**Preventing initiatives**: corporate-speak can neutralize potentially disruptive new ideas before they become threatening. Practical guide to using jargon, committees, and process to slow or redirect unwanted management enthusiasm [^p01].

## Section II: The Strange Business of Software

**The Walrus and the Manager**: uses Carroll's poem as allegory for the dynamics between large corporate clients and small software suppliers. The Walrus (corporate) lures the Oysters (small suppliers) with promises of synergistic partnership. "The oyster who neglects the natural predatory instincts of the walrus does so at his peril" [^p03].

**Clinging to the Flotsam**: on the difficulty of keeping up with technology change when the skills that made you successful quickly become obsolete. The developer who built a career on a platform that has since been deprecated [^p03].

**The Time Bomb**: Phil Factor describes writing subtle anti-piracy protections into software — including time-delayed "bombs" that activate long after a pirate believes they have successfully cracked the code. The Elbonian payroll disaster: counterfeit copies of payroll software, released across an entire country simultaneously, triggered a national payment failure. Resolution required a large check and an "agreeable sushi dinner" [^p06].

## Section III: Software Projects — the Good, the Bad and the Pitiful

**Secrets of successful IT projects**: only 1 in 4 IT projects meets its schedule, budget, and functional goals (per the 1994 Standish CHAOS report). Phil Factor's pattern for the successful outliers:
- Decide what needs to be done; get universal buy-in; then enforce rigid change control.
- Don't be a pioneer — use technology you know will work.
- Code, then recode — throw away the first working version and rewrite it cleanly.
- Keep management visibility low ("the best projects ran out of the way of senior management") [^p04].

The most successful department in one multinational was secretly running COBOL with a Sybase backend — disguising it behind a modern windowing interface. Nobody senior knew. The lesson: boring, proven technology and low profile beats innovation theatre [^p04].

**Offshoring satire (Had offshoring been responsible for Hamlet)**: a Shakespeare parody about offshore project delivery. The "Moorish Project Manager" delivers a tragedy when commissioned for a comedy, citing requirements misalignment, communication failures, and a fundamental inability to produce what was actually wanted despite a polished sales proposal full of "spearhead our business growth" language [^p09].

---

## Section IV: Hiring, Firing, and Other Acts of Villainy

**IT agencies** (recruitment agencies): Phil Factor characterizes IT recruitment agencies as having "the morals of ferrets." Common tactics described: posting fictitious job ads to harvest CVs for their database; mining referees sections of CVs for business development leads; pressuring candidates to inflate CVs [^p07].

**The hiring conundrum**: the interview process optimizes for impression management, not actual technical capability. Smart-sounding candidates who cannot write working code routinely pass interviews; the only reliable filter is a technical test done in the room [^p07].

---

## Section V: Counterfactuals and Parody

A series of "what if" essays applying modern corporate dysfunctions to historical situations:
- **"Had offshoring been responsible for Hamlet"** (§III above) — comedy of requirements misalignment.
- Similar treatments of other historical and literary scenarios, using the distancing effect to comment on present-day IT culture [^p09].

---

## Section VI: Hiccoughs in the Working Day

**Maintenance contracts**: the cautionary tale of calling for hardware replacement on Christmas Eve, being told delivery will take ten days under a "two-hour response" SLA. The vendor's position: "We did respond within two hours. We responded rapidly and efficiently — by telling you it would take ten days." Lesson: never sign a maintenance agreement that does not specify what constitutes a response [^p11].

---

## Key Themes

1. **Information is power in corporate environments** — understanding and using organizational intelligence beats technical excellence alone.
2. **Boring technology ships** — proven stacks with low management attention produce better outcomes than innovative but risky choices.
3. **Change control is the discipline that separates successes from failures** — scope creep, not technical difficulty, kills most projects.
4. **Vendor relationships are adversarial by default** — agencies, contractors, and maintenance suppliers have different incentives than the organization they serve.
5. **The gap between what management hears and what developers experience is structural, not accidental** — bridging it requires translation, not just better communication.
