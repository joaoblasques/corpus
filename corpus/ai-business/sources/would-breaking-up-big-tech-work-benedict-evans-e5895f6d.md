# Would Breaking Up 'Big Tech' Work? — Benedict Evans

Source: ben-evans.com, 2020. Ingested to [/ai-business/platform-dynamics.md].

A structural argument about antitrust remedies for big tech. Network effects behave like natural monopolies: dominance persists not because of anti-competitive behavior but because of product mechanics. Market size determines how many networks can coexist.

AT&T split (1982) created competition in long-distance and equipment, but local access remained a natural monopoly. Separating Instagram from Facebook doesn't change Instagram's network effects. "You don't use WhatsApp because Facebook owns it." The "break them up and they'll compete with each other" argument is "magical thinking" — they would be on the wrong side of network effects.

The alternative to breakups is regulation targeting internal mechanics: EU local loop unbundling (incumbents below 50% DSL share in most European countries), EU roaming price caps, credit card interchange rate caps. CMA approach to Google/Facebook: requiring Google to share click/query data with rivals; restricting Google's default-position sales; requiring Facebook to offer contact-finding to third-party platforms. Complication: technology markets change fast — Microsoft lost its dominance not to antitrust but to smartphones.[^1]
